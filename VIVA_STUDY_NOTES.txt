================================================================================
SECTION 1: THE PROBLEM
================================================================================

WHAT ARE WE DOING?
  A manufacturer operates across multiple warehouses and sells products grouped
  into categories. They need to know HOW MUCH to produce each month so they
  don't run out of stock or overproduce. This is a DEMAND FORECASTING problem.

GRANULARITY:
  We forecast at the level of: Warehouse x Product_Category, monthly.
  Example: "How many units of Category_019 will Warehouse J need in August?"

TOTAL SERIES: 33 (warehouse–category combinations that meet our quality filter)

OUTPUT: Monthly demand forecast, up to 6 months ahead.

WHY IS THIS HARD?
  - Demand can be lumpy (some months near zero, some very high)
  - Multiple series with very different scales (thousands to millions)
  - Limited history (only ~5 years monthly = 60 data points per series)
  - No external signals (no prices, promotions, or economic data)

================================================================================
SECTION 2: THE DATASET
================================================================================

SOURCE: Kaggle — "Forecasts for Product Demand"
RAW SIZE: 1,048,575 rows (about 1 million order records)
COLUMNS: Product_Code, Warehouse, Product_Category, Date, Order_Demand

DATE RANGE IN DATA: roughly 2011–2017
DATE RANGE WE USE: January 2012 – December 2016
  WHY NOT 2011 AND 2017? Those years are almost empty (very few records).
  Including them would create sparse boundaries that corrupt lag features.

WHAT ONE ROW MEANS:
  One row = one order. Same product can appear multiple times on the same day.
  These are ORDER quantities, not actual consumer demand — important limitation.

SECTION 3: DATA CLEANING (pipeline.py → clean() function)
================================================================================

STEP 1 — Bracketed Negatives
  PROBLEM: Values like "(100)" are accounting notation for negative 100.
           pandas reads these as strings, not numbers.
  FIX: Detect rows where Order_Demand contains "(", strip the brackets,
       convert to number, multiply by -1.
  COUNT: 10,469 such values found and fixed.

STEP 2 — Missing/Unparseable Dates
  PROBLEM: Some date strings could not be parsed by pandas.
  FIX: Use errors="coerce" when parsing dates → bad dates become NaT → drop those rows.
  COUNT: 11,239 rows dropped.

STEP 3 — Exact Duplicate Rows
  OBSERVATION: 113,064 exact duplicate rows found.
  DECISION: KEPT (not dropped).
  REASON: The same product can legitimately be ordered twice on one day
          (verified: duplicates are overwhelmingly round-number quantities,
          consistent with real repeat orders, not data entry errors).

STEP 4 — Negative Demand (Returns)
  OBSERVATION: 5,899 rows have negative Order_Demand after fixing bracketed values.
  DECISION: KEPT as-is.
  REASON: Negative demand = product returns. These affect true net demand
          and should be included in monthly aggregation.

STEP 5 — Date Filter
  Keep only rows from 2012-01-01 to 2016-12-31.
  ROWS REMAINING: 1,036,643

================================================================================
SECTION 4: AGGREGATION (pipeline.py → monthly_series() function)
================================================================================

GOAL: Convert raw order rows into a clean monthly time series per series.

STEPS:
  1. Create a "Month" column = first day of the month (Period → Timestamp).
  2. Group by (Warehouse, Product_Category, Month) and SUM Order_Demand.
  3. Create a full calendar grid: every month from Jan 2012 to Dec 2016.
  4. For each series, REINDEX to the full grid and fill missing months with 0.
     (A month with no orders = zero demand, not missing data.)

SERIES FILTERING:
  Only keep series that meet BOTH criteria:
    - At least 36 ACTIVE MONTHS (months with demand > 0)
    - At least 1,000,000 TOTAL DEMAND over the period
  REASON: Series with sparse data or tiny volume are too noisy to forecast
          reliably. They would inflate error metrics without providing value.
  RESULT: 33 series remain.

================================================================================
SECTION 5: FEATURE ENGINEERING (pipeline.py → make_features() function)
================================================================================

KEY PRINCIPLE: NO DATA LEAKAGE.
  Every feature for predicting month t uses ONLY data from month t-1 or earlier.
  This mimics how you'd use the model in production (you never have future data).

FEATURE LIST (13 total):

  LAG FEATURES (5):
    lag_1  = demand 1 month ago
    lag_2  = demand 2 months ago
    lag_3  = demand 3 months ago
    lag_6  = demand 6 months ago
    lag_12 = demand 12 months ago  ← captures yearly seasonality

  ROLLING WINDOW FEATURES (3):
    All computed starting from lag_1 (shifted by 1 first, then rolling):
    roll_mean_3 = mean of last 3 months (t-1, t-2, t-3)
    roll_mean_6 = mean of last 6 months
    roll_std_3  = standard deviation of last 3 months (captures volatility)

  CALENDAR FEATURES (5):
    month   = month number (1–12)
    quarter = quarter (1–4)
    month_sin = sin(2π × month/12)
    month_cos = cos(2π × month/12)
    [year is created but NOT included in model features]

WHY CYCLICAL ENCODING (sin/cos)?
  Plain month integers treat December (12) and January (1) as far apart.
  But they are adjacent months. sin/cos wraps the calendar into a circle:
  Dec → Jan is a small step, not a jump from 12 back to 1.
  This helps the model learn winter/summer patterns correctly.

WHY LOG-TRANSFORM FEATURES?
  Series range from ~4,000 total demand (small) to ~287 million (Category_019).
  Without transformation, the model treats a 1,000-unit error on a small series
  the same as on a huge series. Log-transform compresses the scale so
  ONE MODEL can handle all 33 series fairly.
  Applied to: all lag and rolling features, AND the target (Demand).
  Formula: log1p(x) = log(1 + x)  [handles zeros safely]
  Inverse: expm1(x) = e^x - 1     [used to convert predictions back]

WHAT HAPPENS AFTER make_features()?
  Rows where lag or rolling features are NaN (the first ~12 months of each series,
  where we don't have enough history yet) are dropped with dropna().

================================================================================
SECTION 6: TRAIN / TEST SPLIT
================================================================================

METHOD: TIME-BASED SPLIT (never shuffle time series data)
  Training set: Jan 2012 – Jun 2016
  Test set:     Jul 2016 – Dec 2016  (last 6 months = "hold-out")

WHY NOT RANDOM SPLIT?
  Shuffling would let the model "see" future data during training (leakage).
  For example, a shuffled test set might include Jan 2015 while the training
  set includes Dec 2015 — the model would have been trained on the future.

================================================================================
SECTION 7: MODELS (pipeline.py → get_models() function)
================================================================================

BASELINES (no ML, simple rules):
  1. Naive (last month)     — predict = lag_1 (whatever demand was last month)
  2. Seasonal Naive (12mo)  — predict = lag_12 (same month last year)
  3. Moving Average (3mo)   — predict = roll_mean_3 (average of last 3 months)

  Baselines are essential to check: is our ML model actually better than
  something trivial? If not, the ML model isn't worth the complexity.

ML MODELS (all from scikit-learn):
  1. Linear Regression     — no regularization, simple baseline
  2. Ridge Regression      — Linear Regression + L2 regularization (alpha=10)
                             + StandardScaler (features normalized first)
  3. Random Forest         — 300 decision trees, min_samples_leaf=3
  4. Gradient Boosting     — 300 estimators, learning_rate=0.05, max_depth=3
  5. Hist Gradient Boosting — histogram-based GBM, 300 iterations, lr=0.05
                              (faster than GBR, handles missing values natively)

HOW MODELS ARE TRAINED:
  - Input X: the 13 features (log-transformed demand-scale ones via prep_X())
  - Target y: log1p(Demand) — train on log scale
  - Predict: model.predict(X) → expm1() → clip to ≥ 0

ALL 33 SERIES ARE TRAINED TOGETHER (global model):
  One model sees all series. Series identity is implicitly captured by the
  feature values themselves (different lag patterns per series).

================================================================================
SECTION 8: EVALUATION METRICS
================================================================================

PRIMARY METRIC: WAPE % (Weighted Absolute Percentage Error)
  Formula: WAPE = Σ|actual - predicted| / Σ|actual| × 100

  WHY WAPE?
    - MAPE divides each error by that month's actual demand. If a month has
      near-zero demand, one small absolute error becomes a huge percentage error.
      WAPE avoids this by dividing by the TOTAL actual demand (sum).
    - It's also intuitive: "our total error is X% of total actual demand."
    - Lower is better. 0% = perfect.

SECONDARY METRICS:
  MAE  (Mean Absolute Error)    — average absolute error in original units
  RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) — penalizes large errors more
  sMAPE (Symmetric MAPE)        — alternative percentage error, symmetric
  R²   (R-squared)              — proportion of variance explained (closer to 1 = better)

================================================================================
SECTION 9: CROSS-VALIDATION (train.py)
================================================================================

METHOD: Rolling-Origin Cross-Validation (3 folds)
  Each fold:
    - Training: all data before the cutoff
    - Validation: the 6 months after the cutoff
  Cutoffs used: months[-18], months[-12], months[-6]
  (Expanding window: each fold has MORE training data than the previous)

WHY NOT K-FOLD CV?
  K-fold shuffles data. For time series, that causes leakage. Rolling-origin
  CV always trains on the past and validates on the future — exactly how the
  model will be used in production.

CV RESULTS (mean WAPE % over 3 folds):
  Gradient Boosting        13.2%  ← WINNER (lowest mean)
  Random Forest            13.77%
  Hist Gradient Boosting   14.47%
  Ridge Regression         14.59%
  Moving Average (3mo)     14.86%
  Linear Regression        15.38%

================================================================================
SECTION 10: HYPERPARAMETER TUNING (train.py)
================================================================================

MODEL TUNED: HistGradientBoostingRegressor (best family for raw performance)

APPROACH: Manual grid search on a separate inner validation window
  (not the final test set, to avoid double-dipping)
  Inner window: months[-12] to months[-6] (6-month validation period)

GRID (12 combinations):
  learning_rate    : 0.03, 0.05, 0.1
  max_iter         : 200, 400
  min_samples_leaf : 10, 20

RESULTS (top 3):
  lr=0.05, max_iter=200, min_samples_leaf=10  → val WAPE 16.32%  ← BEST
  lr=0.03, max_iter=200, min_samples_leaf=10  → val WAPE 16.44%
  lr=0.03, max_iter=400, min_samples_leaf=10  → val WAPE 16.68%

OUTCOME: Tuned HGB still didn't beat GBR in rolling-origin CV, so
         Gradient Boosting remained the champion.

SECTION 12: CHAMPION MODEL SELECTION
================================================================================

CHAMPION: Gradient Boosting
CONFIG:   GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=300, learning_rate=0.05,
                                    max_depth=3, random_state=42)

SELECTION LOGIC:
  Champion = model with LOWEST MEAN WAPE across rolling-origin CV folds.
  NOT chosen by hold-out performance alone.

THE KEY QUESTION YOU WILL BE ASKED:
  "HGB has better hold-out WAPE (10.3%) than GBR (11.8%). Why pick GBR?"

  ANSWER:
  1. Hold-out is ONE window (Jul–Dec 2016). It could be lucky/unlucky.
  2. CV averages over THREE windows at different points in time.
  3. On CV: GBR mean = 13.2%, HGB mean = 14.47% — GBR is more consistent.
  4. HGB also has higher CV variance (std 3.74 vs 2.62), meaning it's less stable.
  5. A model that generalizes reliably across time is more trustworthy in
     production than one that happens to be best on one specific window.

AFTER SELECTION:
  All models are refit on THE FULL DATASET (training + test combined) before
  saving to artifacts/. This gives the app models trained on maximum history.

EASY TO FORECAST (low WAPE):
  Whse_J|Category_019 → 6.75%   (biggest series: 287M actual demand)
  Whse_J|Category_005 → 6.97%

         is 287M units out of the total, which is ~83% of all volume).

SECTION 14: RECURSIVE MULTI-STEP FORECASTING (pipeline.py → forecast_future())
================================================================================

STRATEGY: Recursive (also called "MIMO with feedback")

HOW IT WORKS (step by step for horizon=3):
  Step 1: Take the last known demand history for the series.
          Compute lag_1, lag_2, ... lag_12, roll_mean_3, roll_mean_6, roll_std_3
          from real historical data. Predict month t+1.
  Step 2: Append predicted t+1 as if it were real. Recompute features.
          Predict month t+2 using those features (lag_1 is now the t+1 prediction).
  Step 3: Append t+2 prediction. Recompute. Predict t+3.

LIMITATION: Errors compound. An overestimate at t+1 becomes an input for t+2,
            which may then overestimate t+2, which feeds into t+3, etc.
            Uncertainty grows with forecast horizon. Beyond 3 months, forecasts
            become increasingly unreliable.

UNCERTAINTY BAND IN APP:
  The app shows a shaded band around the forecast:
    Lower = Forecast × (1 - WAPE/100)
    Upper = Forecast × (1 + WAPE/100)
  Where WAPE = the per-series hold-out WAPE for that specific series.
  This is a HEURISTIC (not a formal statistical interval) but gives intuition
  about expected error for that particular series.

SECTION 16: LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

1. SHORT HISTORY
   Only ~5 years of monthly data = ~60 data points per series.
   Yearly seasonality (lag_12) is weakly captured with so few years.

2. ORDERS ≠ TRUE CONSUMER DEMAND
   The data shows what was ordered, not what consumers actually wanted.
   Stock-outs, bulk orders, promotions, and pricing changes are invisible.
   This means the model learns "historical ordering patterns", not true demand.

3. DOMINATED BY ONE CATEGORY
   Category_019 in Whse_J has 287 million units = ~83% of all volume.
   The overall WAPE of 11.8% mostly reflects how well we predict this one series.
   Smaller series have WAPE up to 121% (effectively useless forecasts).

4. RECURSIVE ERROR COMPOUNDING
   Multi-step forecasts beyond ~3 months degrade significantly because each
   prediction feeds back as an input for the next prediction.

5. INTERMITTENT DEMAND
   Series with many zero-demand months are fundamentally hard to forecast with
   lag-based ML models. Specialized methods (Croston's method, zero-inflated
   models) would be more appropriate but were not implemented.

6. NO EXTERNAL FEATURES
   No pricing, promotional calendar, macroeconomic indicators, or weather data.
   All features are purely historical demand patterns.

SECTION 17: CONCEPTS YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND
================================================================================

WHAT IS WAPE?
  Σ|y - ŷ| / Σ|y| × 100
  "What percentage of total actual demand is our total absolute error?"
  Example: Total demand = 1,000,000. Total absolute error = 118,000. WAPE = 11.8%.

WHAT IS sMAPE?
  Mean of [ |y - ŷ| / ((|y| + |ŷ|) / 2) ] × 100
  Symmetric version of MAPE. Denominator uses average of actual and predicted,
  so over- and under-forecasting are penalized equally.

WHAT IS R²?
  1 - (SS_residual / SS_total). Proportion of variance explained by the model.
  R² = 1.0 = perfect. R² = 0 = model is no better than predicting the mean.
  All our models: R² ≈ 0.98–0.99 (very high because Category_019 dominates
  and is easy to fit — don't be fooled by this number).

WHAT IS GRADIENT BOOSTING?
  An ensemble method that builds trees SEQUENTIALLY.
  Each new tree corrects the RESIDUAL ERRORS of the previous trees.
  Parameters:
    n_estimators = number of trees (300)
    learning_rate = shrinkage factor (0.05 — small = slower but more stable)
    max_depth = maximum depth of each tree (3 — shallow trees → regularization)

WHAT IS HISTGRADIENTBOOSTING?
  A faster variant of gradient boosting. Instead of exact splits, it bins
  feature values into histograms (like LightGBM). Much faster on large datasets.
  Also natively handles missing values (unlike standard GBR).

WHAT IS RIDGE REGRESSION?
  Linear regression with L2 regularization: adds λΣw² to the loss function.
  Penalizes large weights → prevents overfitting. alpha=10 here.
  Needs StandardScaler because L2 penalty is scale-sensitive.

WHAT IS ROLLING-ORIGIN CV?
  A cross-validation strategy for time series where:
  - You always train on the PAST and validate on the FUTURE.
  - The training window EXPANDS with each fold (more data each time).
  - Avoids the leakage of k-fold CV for sequential data.

WHAT IS A GLOBAL MODEL?
  One model trained on data from ALL series simultaneously (vs. one model per series).
  Pros: more training data, can learn patterns across series.
  Cons: series with very different dynamics might confuse each other.
  Here: log-transformation normalizes across scales, making one model viable.

WHAT IS LOG1P / EXPM1?
  log1p(x) = log(1 + x)   → safe for x=0 (log(0) is undefined, log(1)=0)
  expm1(x) = e^x - 1      → inverse of log1p
  Used to transform demand to log scale (and back) for training and prediction.

SECTION 20: LIKELY VIVA QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS
================================================================================

Q: Why did you use WAPE instead of MAPE?
A: MAPE divides each error by the monthly actual demand. If a month has near-zero
   or zero demand, MAPE blows up to infinity. Our series have months with zero
   demand (especially small series), so MAPE is unreliable. WAPE divides by the
   SUM of all actual demand, which is always positive and meaningful.

Q: How did you handle the fact that different series have very different scales?
A: Two ways: (1) Log-transforming all demand-scale features and the target before
   training, so the model operates in a compressed, comparable space. (2) WAPE
   itself handles this — by computing error as a fraction of total demand, it's
   scale-independent.

Q: Why is R² so high (0.98–0.99) even for weaker models?
A: Because Category_019 in Whse_J alone accounts for ~83% of total volume.
   It has a very stable, predictable trend. Any model that predicts it reasonably
   well will have a high R². R² is misleading here — WAPE tells a more honest story.

Q: What is recursive forecasting and what's the risk?
A: For multi-step forecasts (predicting multiple months ahead), each predicted
   value is fed back as a lag feature for the next prediction. Risk: errors
   compound — a bad prediction at t+1 corrupts inputs for t+2, t+3, etc.
   Uncertainty grows with forecast horizon.

Q: Why did you choose Gradient Boosting over Hist Gradient Boosting when HGB
   has better hold-out performance?
A: Hold-out is a single 6-month window and may not be representative. We chose
   by rolling-origin CV (3 folds). On CV, GBR has mean WAPE 13.2% vs HGB's
   14.47%, and lower standard deviation (2.62 vs 3.74). GBR is more consistently
   good across different time windows — that's what matters in production.

Q: What would you do differently with more data or time?
A: (1) Add external features: promotions, pricing, macroeconomic indicators.
   (2) Try sequence models (N-BEATS, Temporal Fusion Transformer) that are
   designed for time series and can learn complex temporal patterns.
   (3) Handle intermittent demand separately with Croston's method.
   (4) Use conformal prediction for proper uncertainty quantification.
   (5) More hyperparameter tuning with proper nested CV.

Q: What is the difference between GBR and HGB in scikit-learn?
A: Both are gradient boosting, but HGB uses histogram-based split finding
   (like LightGBM) — it bins feature values before building trees. This makes
   it significantly faster on large datasets and allows it to handle missing
   values natively. The tradeoff is slight approximation in split points.

Q: Why did you refit models on the full dataset before saving?
A: The train/test split was only for EVALUATION purposes (to get honest error
   estimates). Once we've chosen the best model, we retrain it on ALL available
   data so the final deployed model has learned from as much history as possible.
   The test set is no longer "unseen" once we've made our model choice.

Q: How does the production planning view in the app work?
A: User inputs a safety stock percentage and current inventory. The app adds
   the safety stock buffer to forecasted demand (Target = Forecast × (1 + safety%))
   then computes how much needs to be produced each month accounting for current
   stock on hand (Production = max(0, Target_cumulative - Stock)).

Q: Why did you keep exact duplicate rows?
A: They represent real repeat orders on the same day. The same customer (or
   different customers) ordering the same product on the same day is a legitimate
   business event. Removing them would undercount actual demand. The pattern
   (mostly round numbers) confirms these are genuine orders, not data entry errors.
